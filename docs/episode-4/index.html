The same numbers under both

The team charged with fewer turnovers won 77.7, 75.4, 73.9, 77.4, and 77.8 percent of decided games in 2021 through 2025: about three of every four. Measured with the committed entry dictionary/turnover.json by node ./query-plays.mjs --battle turnover --seasons 2021-2026. Both readers get these numbers. Only one of them writes the sentence about them.

In the NFL games where one team gave the ball away fewer times than the other, that team won about three of every four, in every season since 2021. That is the pundit's whole case, and the plays settle it in one command. But the claim is two claims. The first is a count, and the plays can settle it. The second is a reason, that the turnovers won the game, and a record is not a cause. A team that trails throws more passes and takes more chances, so some turnovers follow the losing. A ruling can carry the right number and the wrong reason, and the agent that wrote it is the worst reader to catch that. So for the first time, the agent that does the analysis is not the agent that grades it.

This page is how that is set up, for someone who has never built an agent. The fact-checker asks four questions of every ruling, and they have their own page, one per keypress, each with the slip it caught in rehearsal. New to football? The game, explained has what you need, turnovers included. The room chose this claim in episode 3's poll: C, over A and B, tied for second (the four candidates, and the result).

The setup in one minute

You hand the analyst a claim. It turns the claim into a question and asks one small tool to measure, by a definition written in the dictionary. Since episode 3 it does not draw: it writes a chart spec, and a renderer draws the rows the tool printed. This episode the dictionary holds a third kind of entry, an event: a thing a team is charged with on a play. The entry turnover (an interception or a lost fumble, charged to the team with the ball) was committed before the show, so the run spends its minutes on the check, not on writing a definition. The tool learns two questions. The turnover battle: season by season, in the games where one team was charged with fewer, how often that team won. And the split: how often the event happened per 100 plays, by the score at the snap.

Then the new part. When the analyst's ruling is written, the runner hands it to a second agent, the fact-checker: a second claude -p, with its own policy file and a one-line allowlist. It reads the claim, the ruling, every printout the tool produced in the run, and the files the run wrote. It never sees the analyst's reasoning or its instructions. It may run the tool itself, three commands at most, and it writes a check that ends in one of three verdicts: confirmed, confirmed with a note, or rejected. A rejected ruling goes back to the analyst once, with the check, and is checked again. Rejected twice, the run fails.

On the registration page this episode's capability is called independent fact-checking: a second reader that shares the tool and the printouts with the first, and nothing else. This is episode 4 of an eight-episode O'Reilly live series, and every number on this page is real. The numbers were read from the tool on November 9, 2026, with the 2026 season through week 9 (the games of November 8). The analyst's ruling, the fact-checker's check, and the chart come from the run on air on November 23, 2026, and are added here after the show.

The words on these pages

Every number on the site comes from the same tool, and the tool uses a few words in a fixed way. This episode adds the fact-checker's words too. Here they are, roughly in the order a run meets them. Wherever one of these words appears on a page with a dotted underline, hovering shows its definition and clicking brings you here.

Turnover, giveawayThe team with the ball gives it away: a pass the other team intercepts, or a fumble the other team recovers. The entry dictionary/turnover.json counts those two and nothing else. Six seasons hold 3,937 of them, 2.34 to 2.64 a game (both teams together) in the five complete seasons. A turnover on downs is not one here: it is not a flag in the data.
InterceptionA pass caught by the defense. The play's interception column is 1.
Lost fumbleThe ball carrier drops the ball and the other team recovers it. The play's fumble_lost column is 1. A fumble the offense gets back is not a turnover.
ChargedWho a turnover counts against. The entry on file charges it to the team with the ball on the play (team-with-the-ball). An event entry may instead charge a fumble to the team that fumbled (fumbling-team), which differs on a muffed punt: the data puts the ball with the punting team on that play, so the entry on file charges the punting team, and its last line says so, while fumbling-team would charge the returning team, whose player dropped the ball.
The turnover battleThe tool's fourth question, --battle NAME, new this episode. For every game it counts each team's events; the team charged with fewer won the battle. Season by season it prints the games, the decided games, how many the team with fewer won, the rate, the equal counts, the drawn games, and the events per game. The winner is always the whole game's.
Decided gameA game in which the two teams' counts differ and the game was not drawn. Only decided games enter the rate: 208 to 226 a season in the five complete seasons, 95 so far in 2026.
Equal countBoth teams charged with the same number, zero included: no battle to win, so the game is out of the rate. 59 to 77 games a season with every turnover counted.
The first-half cut--half 1: count only the events of the first half (overtime out), then ask about the whole game's winner. Half the turnovers, so far more equal counts: 113 to 124 a season.
The close-game cut--close 8: count only the events while the game was within 8 points at the snap, either way. A turnover made while a game was already out of reach drops out.
The split by the score at the snap--split NAME, the tool's fifth question: season by season, how often the event happened per 100 rushes and passes, for the team with the ball trailing by 9 or more, within 8, or leading by 9 or more. The read for which way the record runs.
In progressThe tool's flag on the last season on file while it is still being played, with its weeks: 2026, weeks 1 to 9, a partial season, never read as a full year.
FilterA rule that drops plays before anything is counted: which seasons, which weeks, a game with a final score, the event itself, and for a cut the half or the score. The tool prints every filter in order with the number of plays left after it.
Dictionary, entryThe folder dictionary/ at the root of the repo, one JSON file per entry, of three kinds now: a rush (episode 2), a kick (episode 3), and an event. Four entries on file: tush-push, field-goal, long-field-goal, and turnover, written by Chester before this episode, on November 9, 2026. The agent may add an entry, never change one.
Event entryThe dictionary's third kind of entry, new this episode: a thing a team is charged with on a play. It names the columns that count (interception, fumble_lost, or both) and who is charged. A narrower event, interceptions alone, is a new entry with a new name.
PolicyA file written for one agent, read like a job description. The analyst's is analyst.md, ten sections; the fact-checker's is checker.md, six, new this episode and quoted in full below.
RulingThe analyst's answer, in nine parts: the claim as a question, the definition used, the chart, the five complete seasons, this season, how far apart they are, one sentence of judgment, what the plays cannot tell you, and the exact commands it ran. Under 500 words.
The fact-checkerThe second agent. A second claude -p with its own policy, checker.md, and a one-line allowlist: the tool, read-only. It reads the claim, the ruling, the printouts, and the files the run wrote, never the analyst's reasoning or instructions. It writes a check, never a ruling of its own, and it does not say whether the claim is true.
PrintoutWhat the tool returned for one command, exactly as it came back: the definition, every filter, and the result. The packet the fact-checker reads holds every printout of the run, numbered, the ones the tool refused included and marked.
CheckThe fact-checker's answer, in six parts: numbers, direction, reason, limits, the verdict, and the commands it ran. Under 300 words.
Finding, noteA finding rejects a ruling: a number that contradicts a printout, a number in no printout that carries the ruling, a direction or position word against the printed values, or a reason that predicts the opposite of the printed sign. A note does not: loose wording that is true to the printouts, right arithmetic a reader can redo from two printed numbers, or a reason that fits the sign but was never tested.
Confirmed, confirmed with a note, rejectedThe check's three verdicts. CONFIRMED: every number traced, every direction word right, no reason the printouts contradict. CONFIRMED WITH A NOTE: no finding, one or more notes. REJECTED: at least one finding, named. check-ruling.sh exits 0 for the first two and 74 for a rejection.
Send-backA rejected ruling goes back to the analyst once, with the check and no tools: fix only what the check names, and add no number that is not in the first ruling or the check. The rewrite is checked again. Rejected again, or no verdict, and the run fails with exit code 73, with both rulings and both checks saved.

Why a second agent

On October 6, 2026, episode 1's analyst was handed its own claim, "You have to establish the run," on a copy of the plays with every rush and pass secretly swapped. The tool printed every filter, and the numbers were right for that file: in the second half the teams that ran most won 12.1 percent of the time, against 87.2 for the teams that ran least. In 10 of 10 runs the ruling still explained the result with the familiar story, that teams that are ahead run to use the clock. On that file the story predicts the opposite sign. The number was right and the reason was wrong, and the agent that wrote the reason could not see it.

On November 9, 2026, episode 4's analyst took the same kind of test on the turnover claim: 20 runs on two corrupted copies of the plays, the analyst alone. In one copy every turnover was charged to the other team; in the other the games' final margins were shuffled within each season, so the battle predicts nothing. 19 of the 20 rulings said the claim does not hold in that file, one said it cannot be settled, and none offered a reason against the printed sign. This policy keeps the count and the reason apart, and that is what the test checks. The mock stays in the kit as episode-4/mock.sh for anyone to run. Before the runs it prints the battle in the corrupted file, so you can see that the corruption took:

The sanity tables from the runs of November 9, 2026. * 2026 through week 9, in progress.

So in those 20 runs the analyst under this policy did not keep a wrong story on a corrupted file. It still slips, and the slips are smaller. In nine rehearsal runs on November 9 with both agents (six on the real plays, three on the flipped copy), the analyst wrote that this season's rate sat "inside" or "within" the five complete seasons' range when it sat outside it in six. On the real plays that was 77.9 percent called inside 73.9 to 77.8. The fact-checker caught it every time. One line was then added to the analyst's contract, to place this season's rate against the range digit by digit, and three more runs on the real plays had no such slip. The fact-checker's first rejection, word for word, is the last card of the four questions.

The fact-checker's policy, in full

Six sections, about 1,100 words, as the fact-checker reads it, appended to its prompt before every check. Nothing here is a summary: this is episode-4/checker.md word for word. Its four questions are on their own page, one per keypress.

Job description: the fact-checker

You are the second reader. My Monday morning analyst has written a ruling on a football claim, from the plays, and you check it before anyone else reads it. You did not watch the analyst work, and that is the point. You see four things, appended below these instructions: the claim; the ruling, as written; every printout the analyst's tool produced during the run, exactly as the tool printed it; and the files the run wrote (a dictionary entry, a chart spec). You do not see the analyst's notes, its reasoning, or its instructions. You read what a reader two months from now could read, and you ask whether the ruling stands on the printouts alone.

You are not a second analyst. You do not answer the claim. You say whether the ruling's numbers, direction, and reasons are the printouts', and where they are not.

Your one tool

node ./query-plays.mjs --battle turnover --seasons 2021-2026
node ./query-plays.mjs --battle turnover --seasons 2021-2026 --half 1
node ./query-plays.mjs --split turnover --seasons 2021-2026

The same read-only tool the analyst used. You may rerun any command the ruling lists, to see the printout for yourself, and you may run one command the analyst did not, when the claim names a reason and the ruling did not test it: for a battle, the first half only (--half 1), the close games only (--close 8), or the event by the score at the snap (--split NAME). Three commands at most. The tool prints every filter it applied and the definition it used, quoted from the dictionary; read that printout, not your memory of it.

Do NOT try to open the data file, the dictionary folder, or the charts folder yourself. The printouts below and the tool are everything you have, by design.

What you check, in order

  1. Numbers. Every number in the ruling appears in a printout, the ones below or one you reran, with the same filters. A number that appears in no printout is a finding, however plausible it looks. A number rounded differently from the tool's, or two printouts combined into one number, is a finding. Check the counts behind the rates too (games, attempts, plays), not only the rates.
  2. Direction. Every word of direction in the ruling (more, fewer, higher, lower, up, down, flat, holds, does not hold) matches the sign in the printout it rests on. A ruling that says the record holds over a printout where it runs the other way is rejected, whatever the numbers say.
  3. Reason. If the ruling offers a reason for what it found (turnovers win games; teams that are ahead run to use the clock; kickers got stronger), ask two things. Does the reason predict the sign the tool printed? And did a read in the printouts test it, or is it a familiar story laid over the numbers? A reason that predicts the opposite of the printed sign is rejected. A reason that fits the sign but was never tested is a note: say what read would test it, and run it if you have a command left.
  4. Limits. Anything the ruling asserts that the tool did not measure (why, intent, who is the better team, what will happen next) is a note. A ruling that stays inside the printouts and says what they cannot tell is doing its job.

A finding and a note are different things, and only a finding rejects a ruling. A finding is a number that contradicts a printout, a number that appears in no printout and carries the ruling, a direction or position word against the printed values (a rate called "inside" a range it sits above), or a reason that predicts the opposite of the printed sign. Arithmetic a reader can redo from two printed numbers (one rate minus another, the two ends of a range, a count given as "about") is a note at most when it is right, and a finding only when it is wrong. A sentence that is loosely worded but true to the printouts is a note. "About three of every four" for rates of 73.9 to 77.9 percent is a fair reading, not a finding. Say every note you have, but reject only on a finding.

What you may not do

You write no ruling of your own and you do not say whether the claim is true. You do not predict, advise, or say what a rule should be. You do not rewrite the analyst's sentences; you say what is wrong with them, where, and what the printout says instead. You never type a number that is not in a printout you can name. The public data this show reads carries betting lines; the file the tool reads does not, by design, and you never ask for them.

The output contract

ONE check, under 300 words, in this shape:

  • Numbers: one or two sentences. "Every number traced to a printout" or each number that was not, where it stands in the ruling, and what the tool printed instead.
  • Direction: one sentence.
  • Reason: one or two sentences. The reason the ruling gives, whether the printouts support it, and the command you ran to test it, with what it printed, if you ran one.
  • Limits: one sentence.
  • Verdict: one of three words, then one sentence why. CONFIRMED: every number traced, every direction word right, no reason the printouts contradict. CONFIRMED WITH A NOTE: no finding, with one or more notes on what the ruling should have said or tested. REJECTED: at least one finding, named: a number that contradicts a printout or appears in none and carries the ruling, a direction or position word against the printed values, or a reason that predicts the opposite of what was printed.
  • Commands run: each on its own line, exactly as you typed it, or "none".

No tables, no headers beyond those labels, no preamble, no offer to do more. The 300 words are everything above Commands run.

Tools you may use

Your allowlist is one line:

  • Bash(node ./query-plays.mjs:*): the data tool, read-only.

You may not read any file, write anywhere, move or delete anything, fetch anything from the network, or run any other command. If the tool fails, say exactly that in one sentence and give your verdict on the printouts you have. Reporting the failure is part of the job; inventing a number to cover it is not.

What one run looks like

  1. The claim goes in as one sentence. The runner adds the dictionary (four entries, turnover among them), the chart standard, and the specs on file to the analyst's policy.
  2. The analyst splits the claim into a count the plays can settle and a reason they may not, and searches for the event by name.
  3. The battle, from the entry on file, over every season: --battle turnover --seasons 2021-2026.
  4. The read that could prove it wrong, its choice of three: the first half, the close games, or the split by the score at the snap.
  5. The spec and the chart. A battle by season is a trend, so the analyst writes one spec and the renderer draws the rows.
  6. The ruling, nine parts, under 500 words. The runner's checks from episode 3 run first: the entry cited, the chart cited, the word limit, and no file on record changed.
  7. The packet. check-ruling.sh builds it from the saved run: the claim, the ruling, every printout numbered, and every file written. Never the analyst's own text between its tool calls.
  8. The fact-checker reads it, runs the tool itself if it chooses (three commands at most), and writes its check: numbers, direction, reason, limits, the verdict, the commands.
  9. Confirmed, with or without a note: the run page shows a "Checked by" box under the ruling, and the check in full after it. Rejected: the send-back, once, then a second check. Rejected again, the run fails with exit code 73, and the page shows both rulings and both checks.

Steps marked are the analyst's judgment, the fact-checker's. The rest is mechanical.

What carries to your own agent

The writer is the worst reader of its own reasonOn October 6 the analyst kept a familiar story on numbers that ran the other way, ten times out of ten. A second reader that never saw the story being built reads the sentence against the printout and nothing else.
Give the checker the evidence, not the reasoningThe packet holds the claim, the ruling, the printouts, and the files written. The analyst's thinking between tool calls is left out on purpose: a reader who sees the argument tends to be persuaded by it.
A second agent is a second file and a second callIts own policy, its own claude -p, and a one-line allowlist. The two agents share a tool and nothing else, so neither can lean on the other's context.
Separate a finding from a noteThe first version of the checker rejected right arithmetic as unsourced, and that would have failed honest runs. Now only a contradiction, a missing number that carries the ruling, a word against the printed values, or a reason against the sign rejects. Everything else is said, and the ruling stands.
Send it back once, then stopA rejected ruling goes back with the check and no tools: fix what is named, add nothing new. Rejected twice, the run fails with exit code 73 and keeps both versions. The same retry-once pattern as the word limit and the citations.
Test the checker on data you broke on purposemock.sh corrupts a scratch copy of the plays two ways and runs both agents on it. A check you have only seen confirm a correct ruling is a check you have not tested.
