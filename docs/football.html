American football, for people who have never watched it.

Every argument on this site is about NFL football, and many of you watch from countries where "football" means a different game. This page gives you just enough of this one to follow the arguments and the data. The numbers themselves are on the episode pages.

The game in one minute

Two teams have 11 players each on the field. One team has the ball, the offense, and tries to move it down a field 100 yards long and into the end zone, a 10-yard area at the far end. The other team, the defense, tries to stop it. When the offense scores or loses the ball, the teams swap roles.

A game is four quarters of 15 minutes of game clock. The clock stops often, for incomplete passes, timeouts, scores, and more, so a game takes about three hours in real time. Halftime comes after the second quarter. A game tied after four quarters goes to overtime.

Touchdown, 6 pointsCarry the ball into the end zone, or catch a pass there. The scoring team then tries for 1 more point with a short kick, or 2 more points by running or passing into the end zone again from 2 yards out.
Field goal, 3 pointsKick the ball between the goal posts at the back of the end zone.
Safety, 2 pointsRare: the defense scores by tackling the ball carrier in his own end zone.

Plays and downs

The game is a series of separate plays. Each starts with a snap, when the ball is passed back from the ground to start the action, and ends a few seconds later. Between the two teams, a game has about 130 to 160 plays, counting kicks.

The offense gets four tries, called downs, to gain 10 yards. If it gains them, it gets a new first down and four more tries for the next 10 yards. If it falls short after four tries, the ball goes to the other team where the last play ended. The spot where a play starts is the line of scrimmage. The spot the offense has to reach is the line to gain.

Fans say a down as two numbers. "Third and 2" means third down, with 2 yards to go to the line to gain. When the line to gain would be past the goal line, the yards to go are the yards to the end zone, and the down is "goal to go," as in "first and goal."

On fourth down most teams do not use the last try. They punt, kicking the ball far down the field, or, when close enough, kick a field goal. Trying for the yards instead is called going for it. Whether coaches should do it more often is episode 5's argument, "Coaches are cowards on fourth down."

A play ends when the ball carrier is tackled to the ground or goes out of bounds, when a pass hits the ground without a catch, or when someone scores. Each play is one row in the data.

Runs and passes

Every play from the offense starts when the center, the player in the middle of the line, snaps the ball to the quarterback. Then the offense has two choices.

A run, or rushThe quarterback hands the ball to a teammate, usually a running back, who carries it forward. Or the quarterback keeps it and runs himself.
A passThe quarterback throws the ball forward to a teammate, who has to catch it before it touches the ground.

Episode 1 tested "You have to establish the run": the belief that a team that runs the ball a lot early in a game goes on to win. To measure it, the site uses rush share, a team's runs divided by its runs plus passes. A team that ran 30 times and passed 30 times has a rush share of 50 percent.

Where the quarterback stands

The quarterback takes the snap from one of two places. Under center, he stands right behind the center, hands under him, and the ball comes straight back into them. In the shotgun, he stands a few yards back and the ball is snapped to him through the air. The data has one column for this, named shotgun: 1 for shotgun and 0 otherwise. It is the only detail the data carries about where anyone lined up.

With a yard or less to go, a team often calls a quarterback sneak. The quarterback takes the snap under center and pushes straight ahead behind the center.

The tush push is a sneak with teammates lined up behind the quarterback who push him forward. The Philadelphia Eagles made it famous, and fans also call it the brotherly shove, after Philadelphia's nickname, the City of Brotherly Love. In May 2025 the team owners voted on a proposal to ban it: 22 for and 10 against. A rule change needs 24 of the 32 votes, so the ban failed and the play stayed legal.

The play-by-play descriptions never name it, and that is episode 2's whole problem. The agent has to define the play from the columns it has: who carried the ball, the yards to go, shotgun or not, and the down. No column says who pushed. Five definitions shows which parts of a fan's definition the data can see.

Converting

A short-yardage play is one with a yard or two to go. On such a play, converted means the play gained the first down or scored a touchdown. The conversion rate is converted plays divided by plays, so 70 conversions in 100 plays is a rate of 70 percent.

To compare two groups of plays, the site subtracts one group's rate from the other's and calls the result the gap, in points. Here "points" means percentage points: a rate of 70 percent against a rate of 60 percent is a gap of 10 points. That is not a gap of 10 percent, since 10 is one sixth of 60.

Kicking

Episode 3 takes on "Kickers are too good now." Four kinds of kick come up.

Field goalThe ball is snapped to a holder kneeling about 7 yards behind the line of scrimmage, who sets it upright on the ground for the kicker. The distance runs from the spot of the kick to the goal posts at the back of the end zone, so with the line of scrimmage at the 30-yard line the kick is about 47 yards.
Extra pointAfter a touchdown, the same kind of kick with the snap from the 15-yard line, about 33 yards, for 1 point.
KickoffIt starts each half and follows every touchdown and field goal: one team kicks the ball off a tee, deep to the other, which catches it and runs it back.
PuntOn fourth down, the punter drops the ball and kicks it before it lands, to send it far down the field.

The season

The NFL has 32 teams. Each plays 17 regular-season games over 18 weeks, with one week off, from September to early January. Then 14 teams, seven from each of the league's two conferences, play a knockout tournament, the playoffs, which ends with the Super Bowl in February. A season is named for the year it starts, so the 2025 season's Super Bowl was played in February 2026.

The site's data holds the seasons 2021 to 2025, all complete, and the 2026 season so far. The 2026 numbers change as games are played. The week column is the schedule week: 1 to 18 in the regular season, and 19 to 22 for the four rounds of the playoffs. The season_type column says regular season or playoffs, and the agent can filter on it.

Who is who

QuarterbackTakes the snap, throws the passes, and runs the offense. The data has no position column, so the site treats the primary passer, the player with the most pass attempts for his team in that game, as the quarterback.
Running backsLine up behind the quarterback and carry the ball on most runs.
Offensive lineThe five players at the front who block, with the center in the middle.
ReceiversWide receivers and tight ends, who run down the field to catch passes. Tight ends also block.
The defenseEleven players whose job is to tackle the ball carrier, stop passes, and take the ball away.
Head coachRuns the team on game day and makes the big calls, among them whether to punt, kick a field goal, or go for it on fourth down.
RefereeLeads the officials, who place the ball after each play, measure for first downs, and call penalties.

Glossary

The words above, in one place, as this site uses them. The episode pages have their own lists for the words each count uses.

SnapThe start of every play: the center passes the ball back between his legs to the quarterback.
DownOne try. The offense gets four downs to gain 10 yards. The down column holds 1 to 4.
Yards to goThe distance from the line of scrimmage to the line to gain. "Third and 2" is third down with 2 yards to go. The column is ydstogo.
Line to gainThe spot the offense has to reach for a new first down, 10 yards from where the current first down started, or the goal line when that is closer.
First downThe first of a new set of four tries. A play that gains the yards to go earns one; the first_down column is 1 when it did.
ConversionOn a short-yardage play, gaining the first down or scoring a touchdown. The conversion rate is converted plays divided by plays.
RushA run: a player carries the ball forward instead of throwing it.
PassA forward throw by the quarterback to a teammate, who has to catch it before it touches the ground.
Under centerThe quarterback stands right behind the center and takes the ball from his hands. In the data, shotgun is 0.
ShotgunThe quarterback stands a few yards back and the ball is snapped to him through the air. In the data, shotgun is 1.
SneakWith a yard or less to go, the quarterback takes the snap under center and pushes straight ahead.
Tush pushA sneak with teammates behind the quarterback pushing him forward, also called the brotherly shove. The data never names it, so episode 2's agent defines it from the columns.
QuarterbackThe player who takes the snap, throws the passes, and runs the offense.
Primary passerThe quarterback as the data can see him: the player with the most pass attempts for his team in that game. The data has no position column.
TouchdownThe ball carried into the end zone or caught there. Worth 6 points, plus a try for 1 or 2 more.
Field goalA kick between the goal posts at the back of the end zone. Worth 3 points.
PuntA kick on fourth down, dropped from the hands, that sends the ball far down the field to the other team.
TurnoverThe offense loses the ball to the defense: a pass the defense catches (an interception), or a ball the carrier drops and the defense recovers (a fumble). Failing on fourth down is a turnover on downs.
HalftimeThe break after the second quarter. Episode 1 uses the score at halftime as a filter.
Regular seasonThe 17 games each team plays over 18 weeks, September to early January. In the data, season_type is REG.
PlayoffsThe knockout tournament of 14 teams after the regular season, ending with the Super Bowl in February. In the data, season_type is POST.
WeekThe schedule week: 1 to 18 in the regular season, 19 to 22 in the playoffs.
